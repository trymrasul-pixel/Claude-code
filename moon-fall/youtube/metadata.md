What if the Moon suddenly stopped in its orbit, and started falling toward us?

In this simulation we follow the Moon's fall hour by hour, from a quiet Monday midnight to the final seconds. The numbers come from real physics: from a standstill, the Moon would reach Earth in 4 days, 19 hours and 36 minutes. Almost all of that time is spent far away. The last 100,000 km take less than seven hours.

You'll see the tides grow eight times stronger every time the distance halves, coastal cities flooding twice a day, the ground itself rising and falling, and the moment the Moon crosses the Roche limit and starts to tear apart.

🔭 THE SCIENCE BEHIND IT
• Average Earth–Moon distance: 384,400 km. You could line up about 30 Earths in the gap.
• The Moon orbits at about 1 km/s. Take that speed away and it falls straight in. Fall time from rest (Earth + Moon mass): ≈ 4 d 19 h 36 min.
• Tidal force scales with 1/distance³. Halve the distance and tides become 8× stronger.
• Roche limit for the Moon: about 18,400 km. Inside it, Earth's tidal pull beats the Moon's own gravity.
• Impact energy: about 3.6 × 10³⁰ J, nearly ten million times the asteroid that ended the dinosaurs.
• In reality the Moon is drifting away from us by about 3.8 cm per year, measured with lasers bounced off Apollo retroreflectors.

This is a simplified "what if" scenario: nothing in nature could actually stop the Moon in its orbit. Fragment behaviour, tide heights and timings near the end are approximations for storytelling.
